The physician behind Medicinal cuisine

Pedanius Dioscorides (c. 40–90 AD)

A Greek physician from Cilicia whose De Materia Medica described around six hundred plants and a thousand preparations. For some 1,500 years it was the reference on herbs, foods and their virtues, and it now grounds Malcolm’s Medicinal cuisine.

Historical, not medical. The uses below are what Dioscorides recorded nearly 2,000 years ago, shared for culinary and cultural interest. They are not medical advice, and no dish here treats, cures or prevents any illness. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, on medication or have a health condition, speak to a doctor or pharmacist before using herbs in quantity.

His life

  • Born at Anazarbus (Anazarbos) in Cilicia, near Tarsus in modern Turkey, then part of the Roman Empire.
  • Lived about 40–90 AD, under the emperors Nero and Vespasian.
  • Practised as an army doctor with the Roman legions in Greece, Italy, Asia Minor and Provence, collecting and testing plants as he travelled.
  • He wrote his herbal around 64 AD, at the suggestion of his fellow physician Areius, and may have used the library at Alexandria.

His work

  • De Materia Medica (“On medical material”) has five books: aromatics, oils and trees; living creatures, cereals and vegetables; roots; other herbs; then vines, wines and minerals.
  • It describes each plant’s appearance and where it grows, then how to prepare it and what it was used for. That practical focus made it unusually useful.
  • Food sits at its heart: barley water, honey and oxymel, olive oils, lentils, cabbage, garlic, fennel, figs, quinces and wines.

His legacy

  • It was copied for centuries. The illuminated Vienna Dioscorides (Codex Vindobonensis, c. 512 AD) was made for Princess Juliana Anicia.
  • Latin editions were printed from 1478, and Aldus Manutius printed the Greek text in Venice in 1499. Mattioli’s commentaries (1544 onwards) spread it across Renaissance Europe.
  • John Goodyer translated it into English in 1655. It shaped European pharmacy and botany into the 17th century.

How Malcolm cooks “Medicinal”

Tick Medicinal under Cuisines (on its own, or fused with others such as Moroccan), or choose the Dioscorides Inspiration mode. Malcolm then:

  • builds dishes from edible ingredients Dioscorides documented, using ancient methods such as oxymel, honeyed wine, barley gruels, brines and olive-oil dressings, adapted for a modern UK kitchen;
  • cites the entries he draws on, for example “Dioscorides, De Materia Medica II.182: Garlic”;
  • explains the traditional properties as history (“Dioscorides held that…”) and adds allergy and pregnancy cautions where they apply;
  • never uses toxic plants or minerals (hemlock, mandrake, aconite, hellebore, henbane, opium poppy, metals). Rue, pennyroyal and wormwood appear in the index for history only.

Try asking for “something warming for digestion”, “a soothing barley supper” or “cooling summer salad”.
